Reconstruct the mode.
Keep its components coupled.

Matching a growth-rate curve is only part of the task. The required result is the spatial eigenfunction, with its temperature, velocity, flux and pressure information preserved under one amplitude and phase convention.

This page defines the reconstruction and its acceptance criteria. No Kim2013 background front, eigenvalue, eigenfunction or source digitization has been computed in this project yet. Issue6 tracks the implementation. The original study is Kim & Kim, Instability of Evaporation Fronts in the Interstellar Medium.

Figure9 is the primary comparison.

The MU69 example compares temperature and transverse velocity on two different transverse cuts. The reconstruction must produce both curves from the same mode:

  • Temperature panel: δT/Tbackground at y = 0.5Ly.
  • Velocity panel: δvy/vx1 at y = 0.25Ly.
  • Source comparison time: t/tgr = 2; pressure P/kB = 1800 K cm⁻³.
  • Table3 uses Ly = 1.06 pc; Table1 quotes the fastest wavelength as 1.05 pc. Keep that rounding distinction when selecting k.

Compare signs, extrema, zero crossings, relative amplitudes and far-field decay. Digitization must distinguish the source’s analytical solid curves from its simulation dots, and retain axis calibration and reading uncertainty. A common amplitude alignment can test shape and relative amplitude; matching the absolute amplitude after the source’s initially shifted front requires a separate initial-mode projection or linear initial-value calculation.

The origin is fixed by T = √(T1T2). Temperature divided by Tbackground is a pointwise conversion from the solver’s δT/T1, rather than the same plotted variable.

Match the paper’s background model first.

The source uses the corrected KI cooling fit but a constant conductivity κ = 10⁵ erg cm⁻¹ s⁻¹ K⁻¹, with m = 1.37mH, n = ρ/m and γ = 5/3. Its reported saturation pressure is Psat/kB = 2282 K cm⁻³. The existing KI/Iwasaki benchmark instead uses κ = 2500√T and has a different computed saturation pressure. Copying its front unchanged would change the problem.

Reconstruct the steady boundary-value problem and its mass-flux eigenvalue from equations (9)–(13), before assembling the linear operator. Figures2–3 and Table1 provide background comparisons. Report residuals against the rounded source values; do not fit coefficients or redefine the origin to force agreement.

Five state components, one eigenfunction.

xi = x / ell_D1
nu = k ell_D1
sigma = Omega / (k vx1)
state = (J, V, M, Theta, dTheta/dxi)
Theta = temperature Fourier amplitude / T1

AppendixA equations (A7)–(A10) form a five-dimensional first-order problem. The source’s state-vector order differs from the presentation order in (A6); it must be explicit in code. The upstream regular basis contains incompressible and thermal modes. Downstream it contains incompressible, vortex and thermal modes. Match the full state, using the regularity and outgoing-mode conditions of (A11)–(A21).

delta vy / vx1 = Re[ -i V(xi) exp(Omega t + i k y) ]
delta T / Tbackground
  = (T1/Tbackground) Re[ Theta(xi) exp(Omega t + i k y) ]

The growing branch may use real state amplitudes; the transverse-velocity Fourier amplitude still carries the explicit −i factor. This gives the transverse phase relation used by the two Figure9 cuts. Independent component normalizations or phase rotations would destroy that information.

Use the paper’s C1 = 1 convention, or a documented equivalent single gauge. Export all five state components, the basis coefficients and reconstructed physical perturbations, with units and the retained approximation order. The source’s low-Mach/isobaric reduction retains hydrodynamic coupling; it must not be replaced by setting pressure and velocity perturbations to zero or advertised as an exact full-compressible spectrum.

Required verification gates.

  1. Background: converge the thermal front, mass flux and energy balance before solving for perturbations.
  2. Operator: independently check the linearization and heat-loss derivative, and substitute every far-field basis vector into the constant-state equations.
  3. Eigenfunction: retain equation, matching and boundary residuals for all components. Use scale-aware norms without ratios through zero components.
  4. Convergence: enlarge both far fields, refine the spatial representation and tighten tolerances. Both the eigenvalue and the normalized mode shape must converge.
  5. Mode identity: follow the same branch by eigenfunction overlap as k changes. Recover the k = 0 translation mode and long-wave displacement limit without imposing them on finite-k modes.
  6. Source comparison: reconstruct both Figure9 cuts with a common normalization. Validate a representative mode with an independent discretization or small-amplitude linear evolution.
  7. Dispersion: then reproduce Figure5 at P/kB = 1700, 1800 and 1900 K cm⁻³. Its ordinate is ΩellD1/vx1 = νσ, not σ alone. Check (A24), the peak and the cutoff; the downstream vortex tail becomes long as σ approaches zero.

A full nonlinear two-dimensional run is a separate, optional task. The coupled Fourier-mode eigenproblem is the required core.

Source questions remain visible.
